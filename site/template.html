How often appeals courts changed their rulings

When someone appeals a judge's decision, the Illinois Appellate Court affirms it, changes it, or dismisses the appeal. Across Cook County appeals decided since 2015, the court changed the ruling at least in part of the time. Each ballot judge below is compared with that rate for the same mix of criminal, civil and family cases. Only appealed cases count, so this shows how well rulings hold up on review.

Campaign money

What each judge's own campaign committee reported taking in since January 2020, from the Illinois State Board of Elections. Most of this money is from the 2020 elections, when many of these judges first won their seats. Judges running only for retention usually raise money together through the Committee for Retention of Judges in Cook County, which can't be split by judge, so a short or missing bar doesn't mean a judge raised nothing at all.

Where each judge's money came from

Money raised from others, by donor group. Donors are grouped by the employer they reported or by organization name. Illinois reports employers mainly for larger gifts, so "Employer not reported" is often the largest share. Groups are assigned by rules and are approximate: a firm whose donors listed only a title like "Partner" can land under businesses. Law firms aren't split into plaintiffs' and defense firms.

Where these donors give elsewhere

For organizations in these judges' donor lists (companies, unions, PACs and the firms donors work for), their own contributions to other Illinois political committees since 2016, by the recipient's party. Committees with no party listed, often judicial or local races, are shown separately. This counts each organization's own contributions, not personal giving by its employees. Most Illinois political money goes to Democrats, so differences in degree are what stand out.

How to read this

  • Sentencing compares each judge with what the whole court did in cases with the same charge class and offense type, using Cook County State's Attorney records from through . A finding appears only when its 95% range rules out "no difference."
  • The comparison chart places each ballot judge among all 85 Cook County judges with enough sentences. Hover or tap a dot for its numbers, and click a ballot judge to open their profile.
  • Sentencing data has limits. It ends in 2024. It doesn't record criminal history. Most cases end in plea deals, so prosecutors shape many sentences. Judges assigned to specialty courts aren't comparable to general courtrooms. With dozens of judges and several measures each, a few results will look notable by chance.
  • Bar ratings come from the Alliance of Bar Associations (13 groups), the Illinois State Bar Association, and the Chicago Bar Association. Nearly every judge is rated Yes, so a No from any group stands out.
  • The attorney feedback grid sorts each quote from the ISBA evaluation into a topic by its wording. It shows what the evaluation mentions, not a score, and a blank cell means the evaluation didn't discuss that topic.
  • Background and attorney criticism are quotes from each judge's ISBA evaluation. A local AI model pulled them out, and code accepts a quote only if it appears word for word in the evaluation. Checked against 30 hand-labeled evaluations, it made no false claims; it occasionally misses an assignment.
  • Appellate track record comes from every Illinois Appellate Court decision since 2015 in CourtListener's bulk data (Free Law Project), matched to judges by the name in each decision's caption. It counts how often the appeals court changed a ruling at least in part, compared with the countywide rate for the same case types. Only appealed cases are included.
  • Campaign money comes from the Illinois State Board of Elections and covers each judge's own committee since 2020. The page shows totals and donors' employers but not individual donors' names.
  • Nothing here is an endorsement. The page shows what the records say. How to weigh them is up to you.
